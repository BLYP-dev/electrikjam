<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Is An IR?</h2>



<p><strong>IR stands for &#8220;Impulse Response&#8221; and it is the digital measurement of a speaker, microphone, or room. An IR can mimic the sound and situation of many speaker types and microphone options, as well as add in or take out any room noise. Combined with an amp sim, an IR can mimic just about any speaker  and microphone situation you can imagine.</strong></p>



<p></div>



<p></p>



<p>This is by far the most exciting feature in the BOSS Katana MKII V2 Software update, because it can make <em>huge differences</em> in your recording tones.<strong> Before you get too excited, you cannot upload your own IR to the Katana.</strong> While this may seem like a little bit of a bummer, the options that BOSS gives you have a lot of potential. You get:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>5 New Mic Options: Based On Popular Mics</strong></li><li><strong>20 Distance Settings</strong></li><li><strong>10 Mic Position Settings</strong></li></ul>



<p><strong>Cab resonance Is still an option, by the way.&nbsp;</strong>That feature was not removed to make room for the new IR features. So you can mix and match the Cab Resonance if you want,&nbsp;<em>along</em>&nbsp;with the IR options! This gives you a ton of speaker output options for recording.</p>



<p>Recording with the BOSS Katana is never going to be on the level of the <a data-lasso-id="140511" href="https://www.electrikjam.com/what-is-a-line-6-helix/" data-type="post" data-id="4926" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><strong>Helix </strong></a>or its ilk when it comes to these kind of options. <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Recording With The BOSS Katana: Dynamic Mics</h2>



<p><strong>Dynamic microphones</strong> are the &#8220;go to&#8221; option in many studios around the world. These mics do exactly what they declare by name, and by being &#8220;dynamic&#8221;, they are also <strong>multi-purpose</strong>. Dynamic mics have been around for decades, and they are still the choice for most guitarists when it comes to recording. Sometimes, a Dynamic mic will be blended with a Condenser mic to capture a guitar cabinet.</p>



<p>BOSS chose some of the most popular mics in the business in the Tone Studio V2 update, and the two Dynamic options are no surprise. Both of these mics are popular for being versatile and they cover a pretty broad spectrum of frequencies. You really can&#8217;t go wrong with either in Tone Studio, but they are both a little different, albeit the difference is subtle.</p>



<p><strong>The DYN57:</strong> This mic preset is based on the famous Shure SM57. This is one of the most popular microphones in the world, and every studio on the planet has a few of these lying around. The SM57 is a <strong>Dynamic</strong> microphone. Dynamic microphones are known to be robust, and relatively inexpensive compared to other microphones.</p>



<p><strong>The DYN57</strong> is a pretty accurate model of the famous real-life counterpart. Dynamic mics are known to pick up low-midrange, and midrange in general very well. This makes them perfect for recording a guitar, which is a <em>midrange instrument</em>. The<strong> DYN57</strong> Katana setting mimics this by being bright, and having a lot of &#8220;punch&#8221;. This mic option would be perfect for crunch settings, as well as high gain frequencies. The 57 is a great &#8220;all around&#8221; choice for most Katana patches, just like in real life applications.</p>



<p>So if you are trying to get a great rhythm tone, the <strong>DYN57</strong> might be a good choice. This mic option doesn&#8217;t change much when it comes to frequencies. It will not add any &#8220;color&#8221; or personality to your guitar tones, so it makes a great &#8220;default mic&#8221; choice for most users.</p>



<p><strong>The DYN421</strong> on the other hand, offers a different flavor of Dynamic mic when recording with the BOSS Katana. Based on the often-used Sennheiser MD421, this is another dynamic mic that is popular in studios for recording guitar. The MD421 has a slightly larger diaphragm than an SM57 microphone, meaning that it picks up a little more when it comes to frequency.</p>



<p>The <strong>DYN421 </strong>setting on the BOSS Katana reflects this, and it allows a little more treble and bass to your overall guitar tone. The <strong>DYN421</strong> and <strong>DYN57</strong> are both very midrange-focused, but the <strong>DYN421</strong> is going to sound a little more full, since it picks up more frequencies by nature. I find the 421 to be a better option for making lead/solo patches when recording with the BOSS Katana. Because it has a little more character, and pushes the frequencies you need for fast, single note passages. Two of my new solo tones use the <strong>DYN421</strong>.</p>



<p>Both Dynamic mic options in Tone Studio can do just about any style you want. That&#8217;s exactly why their real-life counterparts are used in studios for<em> just about everything</em>. I think that the DYN421 sounds a little &#8220;wider&#8221; to my ears, but you may disagree. If you want a good &#8220;all around&#8221; tone, then try out the Dynamic options first.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Condenser Mic Options</h2>



<p><strong>Condenser Microphones </strong>are a whole different beast compared to a Dynamic mic. Condenser mics are more <em>focused</em> in approach, and they record much quieter than a Dynamic mic. Condenser mics are often used for vocals, but they are just as well known for recording guitars. Condenser microphones are known for being a little more sensitive, and picking up<em> all kinds</em> of frequencies in the spectrum. Condensers are even used to record cymbals and drums sometimes!</p>



<p>The <strong>CDN451</strong> setting is based on the famous AKG C451 small diaphragm Condenser microphone. The <strong>CDN451</strong> is supposed to mimic the airy, almost ambient quality of the real AKG mic. To me, it seems to do this really well, especially if you have it close to the speaker cone in the settings menu (more on that later). The 451 is popular to use with acoustic guitars, and the Katana <strong>Acoustic</strong> setting works well with this mic option.</p>



<p>But the <strong>CDN451</strong> mic option can also be used for solo tones as well. I have tried it out with a few of my high gain solo tones that feature modulation effects, and the 451 seems to pick up the delay and chorus effects with more depth. So this one is a good mic setting for your patches that use a lot of effects. Reverbs, and delays really shine with this IR in my opinion.</p>



<p>The <strong>CDN87 </strong>is based on yet another legendary microphone that every studio probably has several of. The Shure SM87 is a very popular Condenser mic that has been used for as long as I can remember. However, it is usually used primarily for vocals in most studios. But it can also be used for guitars, especially if you&#8217;re trying to &#8220;warm up&#8221; the sound.</p>



<p>The <strong>CDN87</strong> is probably the least used of all the mic choices for me, since I really only use it for my <strong>clean </strong>sounds. This is because of the warm sound it gives most of my patches. I tried it with high gain patches, and it just wasn&#8217;t my thing at all. That being said, it might be exactly what you&#8217;re looking for when it comes to a high gain solo tone, if you boost the treble.</p>



<p>Condenser mic options are great for<strong> layering tracks </strong>if you are recording with the <a href="https://www.electrikjam.com/boss-ga-fc-ex-footswitch-new/" data-lasso-id="155686">BOSS Katana</a>. Since they sound much different than a dynamic mic, they can be used to &#8220;fill in the gaps&#8221; of your mix if you have multiple guitar tracks in a song. <em>At least, that is how I have been using them</em>. YOU might primarily use the Condenser over the Dynamic. The choice really comes down to what sounds good to your ears.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Ribbon Mics</h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" width="1024" height="683" src="https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-Katana-Ribbon-Mic-1024x683.png" alt="Recording with the boss katana" class="wp-image-8482" title="" srcset="https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-Katana-Ribbon-Mic-1024x683.png 1024w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-Katana-Ribbon-Mic-600x400.png 600w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-Katana-Ribbon-Mic-300x200.png 300w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-Katana-Ribbon-Mic-768x512.png 768w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-Katana-Ribbon-Mic.png 1200w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /><figcaption><strong>Royer 121 Ribbon Mic</strong></figcaption></figure>



<p>Ribbon mics are usually very expensive, and there is a good reason that they are. Ribbon mics are used when you want to get the most &#8220;natural&#8221; sound possible. The Tone Studio offers only one Ribbon mic option, but this is because all Ribbon mics really have the same &#8220;character&#8221;, just to varying degrees. They pick up<em> everything</em>, not just what they are pointed at. They also naturally tend to pick up more low end/bass frequencies.</p>



<p>The <strong>RBN121 </strong>is based on the legendary Royer R121 Ribbon mic. Other brands also make a version of the 121, and they are all usually quite expensive. This one was a head scratcher to me when I checked out the BOSS Tone Studio update, because I have usually used Ribbon mics for violins, or for &#8220;room mics&#8221;. Sometimes for vocals as well, but for guitar? Rarely.</p>



<p>That being said, if you are recording with the BOSS Katana, then maybe you can find a better use for the <strong>RBN121</strong> than I did. I found that the <strong>Acoustic</strong> setting sounded really good with this IR. But for most of my electric guitar patches, it just sounded like a blanket was thrown over the cab. I suppose with some EQ, you could work out a great tone, but I didn&#8217;t play with this one as much.</p>



<p>So if you are looking for a really warm sound, that seems a tad muffled, then the RBN121 is the perfect choice. It gets a little more clear the closer you put it to the speaker cone in Tone Studio. But it was still too warm for my tastes. I prefer a big, punchy midrange sound for most of my guitar patches. But maybe you can dial in something awesome!</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Recording With The BOSS Katana: Cone Placement</h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large"><img decoding="async" width="1024" height="683" src="https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-tone-studio-record-3-1024x683.png" alt="Recording with the boss katana" class="wp-image-8483" title="" srcset="https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-tone-studio-record-3-1024x683.png 1024w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-tone-studio-record-3-600x400.png 600w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-tone-studio-record-3-300x200.png 300w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-tone-studio-record-3-768x512.png 768w, https://www.electrikjam.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Boss-tone-studio-record-3.png 1200w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /></figure>



<p>So, this is a feature that I have seen pretty poorly explained on most platforms. In the picture above, I have put a star on both of the speaker cone position controls. These are yet another way to tweak your final output sound from your Katana after you have picked a mic. These can be explained rather quickly, though, with some simple geometry.</p>



<p>Think of the <strong>Distance</strong> and <strong>Position </strong>controls as an almost<strong> X and Y axis</strong> control for the virtual microphone that you have chosen. They do pretty much exactly what they say. The<strong> Distance </strong>is <em>how far away the mic is positioned from the cone of the speaker.</em> The<strong> Position</strong> is more of <em>moving the microphone around the speaker cone.</em> Both of these options can have a huge impact on your tone, as anyone would tell you that has done this in real life.</p>



<p><strong>Distance</strong> is going to matter the most when it comes to the sound of your Katana&#8217;s output. The further away you have the mic, the less of the microphone&#8217;s characteristics you get. So let&#8217;s say you have chosen the <strong>CDN87</strong> as the mic you want to use. The closer you put the mic, the more sound it is going to capture. The further away you put it, the less volume and overall frequency response.</p>



<p>So the <strong>Distance</strong> and<strong> Position</strong> controls are going to vastly affect the final product when it comes to dialing in your tone. The best advice I can give you, is to choose the mic that suits the patch you have made, and then play around with both of these controls until you nail the tone you are after.<strong> For me, I like a close up mic for high gain, and a backed-off mic for clean tones.</strong> But you may like the opposite, so it is completely up to you.</p>

<h3 class="rank-math-question ">Can You Record Directly With The BOSS Katana?</h3>
<div class="rank-math-answer ">

<p>You actually have two different popular methods to when it comes to recording with the BOSS Katana. The MKII series will allow you to record directly with USB, no interface required. The other method is to have an interface, and use the &#8220;Line Out&#8221; from the Katana. Both methods have changed with new features from Tone Studio V2, which now offers Impulse Responses.</p>

<h3 class="rank-math-question ">Can You Use The BOSS Katana as an Interface?</h3>
<div class="rank-math-answer ">

<p>You can, and it is super easy to navigate by using Tone Studio V2. The katana itself acts as an interface when you have your Katana hooked up to your computer and DAW via USB.</p>
