<p><strong><a data-lasso-id="247281" href="https://davidrossmusicalinstruments.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow">David Ross Musical Instruments</a></strong> really impressed us with the <strong><a data-lasso-id="247282" href="/the-david-ross-winter-storm-review-an-awesome-3-in-1-pedal/" data-type="post" data-id="10528" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Winter Strom Pedal </a></strong>just a few months ago. <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="what-is-the-spellbook-pedal">What Is The Spellbook Pedal?</h2>



<p>The Spellbook is a self-oscillating fuzz and distortion guitar pedal that can also be used as a stand-alone synthesizer. In addition to the Volume control, the Start/End control is a bass and treble boost with the center position being flat, and the Middle control is a mid boost and cut with the center position being flat. The Content control changes the note generated by the oscillator, and the Mode switch (I, II, III) chooses the frequency range of the oscillator.</p>



<p></div>



<p></p>



<p></p>



<p>Basically, David Ross seems to be some kind of mad scientist when it comes to pedals. He keeps tweaking standard designs that results in a new pedal that you would never find from a mainstream company. So today, we are going to go over the Spellbook, and talk about how it works.</p>



<p>But if you are new to the idea of oscillation, or modulation effects in general, then perhaps we should go over that first. You probably have a few pedals that use a similar circuit, but not quite like the Spellbook. So let&#8217;s take a look at how the core of this pedal works.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="but-wait-what-is-oscillation-exactly">But Wait, What Is Oscillation Exactly?</h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" width="1024" height="683" src="/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-pedal-2-1024x683.png" alt="Spellbook pedal" class="wp-image-12524" title="" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-pedal-2-1024x683.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-pedal-2-600x400.png 600w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-pedal-2-300x200.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-pedal-2-768x512.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-pedal-2.png 1200w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /></figure>



<p>We are going to talk about this in the way oscillation works in regards to synthesizers, since that is essentially what the Spellbook pedal does. If you already know how all of this works, then go ahead and skip this part, but if you are wondering what this pedal does that is so different, read on.</p>



<p>But we can also look at it in the way that guitar effects like tremolo and vibrato works as well. At the heart of many synthesizer/guitar effects lies the concept of oscillation, a fundamental principle that underlies the creation of diverse sonic textures. In this exploration, we&#8217;ll delve into how oscillation works in a musical context, particularly within synthesizer effects.</p>



<p>Oscillation refers to a repetitive and cyclical motion that occurs over time. In the context of synthesizers, oscillators are electronic circuits or modules that generate audio waveforms with specific frequencies, giving rise to musical tones. These oscillators produce waveform shapes like sine, sawtooth, square, and triangle, each with distinct tonal characteristics.</p>



<p>Two guitar effects that do this, are a good example of what a synthesizer also does. You will probably be familiar with these terms:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Tremolo:</strong> Tremolo is achieved by modulating the amplitude (volume) of an audio signal using an oscillating waveform. The oscillating waveform causes the volume to fluctuate rhythmically, creating a pulsating effect.</li>



<li><strong>Vibrato:</strong> Vibrato introduces pitch modulation by oscillating the pitch of an audio signal around its original frequency. This effect adds a subtle wobble to the sound, reminiscent of a human vocalist&#8217;s natural vibrato.</li>



<li><strong>Wah-wah:</strong> A wah-wah effect employs a sweeping band-pass or low-pass filter that oscillates back and forth. This creates a distinct &#8220;WAH&#8221; sound as the filter opens and closes, allowing certain frequencies to pass while attenuating others.</li>



<li><strong>Phaser:</strong> Phasers use multiple phase-shifted audio signals to create notches and peaks in the frequency spectrum. Oscillating these phase-shifted signals results in the classic swirling and sweeping phaser effect.</li>
</ul>



<p>If you are into synth-wave music, then you are familiar with a saw wave sound. The big, distorted sound that most artists like Carpenter Brut uses is called a &#8220;super saw&#8221; and it is a devastating sound. <strong><a href="/fuzz-vs-distortion/" target="_blank" data-type="post" data-id="6200" rel="noopener" data-lasso-id="247805">This is why fuzz is unique from distortion</a></strong>. Fuzz changes the fundamental <em>shape </em>of the guitar tone, while distortion is based on tube break-up.</p>



<p>So the Spellbook pedal does this waveform shape-shifting effect as well, with three different octave choices to blend with the fuzz signal. I can honestly say that this is the first time I have ever seen this in a guitar pedal. So how does it sound? How does it work?</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="spellbook-pedal-features-and-specs">Spellbook Pedal: Features And Specs</h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" width="1024" height="683" src="/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-Pedal-3-1024x683.png" alt="Spellbook Pedal " class="wp-image-12525" title="" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-Pedal-3-1024x683.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-Pedal-3-600x400.png 600w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-Pedal-3-300x200.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-Pedal-3-768x512.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Spellbook-Pedal-3.png 1200w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /></figure>



<p>The Spellbook pedal has three different modes to choose from, and each one sets the &#8220;note&#8221; range for the pedal. This is controlled by the switch in the middle of the pedal. All three offer a wide range to play around with, and you can change the &#8220;note&#8221; with the <strong>Control </strong>knob.</p>



<p><strong>Mode 1 </strong>probably sounds the &#8220;cleanest&#8221; to me, and it doesn&#8217;t glitch out as much with the lower strings on your guitar. If you tune down, you will get some really cool effects from Mode 1. Likewise, the other two modes also sound pretty cool with<strong><a href="/baritone-guitar-tuning-guide/" target="_blank" data-type="post" data-id="11702" rel="noopener" data-lasso-id="247806"> my guitar in Drop A tuning</a></strong>, glitching out to the ringing note when I let the lowest string ring out.</p>



<p>Depending on which switch position you set the pedal to, you can get some really warbly, synth-like tones from open strings. The more movement you use in your playing, the more &#8220;stable&#8221; the Spellbook remains. </p>



<p>The <strong>Control </strong>knob sets the actual &#8220;note&#8221;, which you can do by ear, or with a tuner pedal. When you are not playing the strings, this is the note that you hear. Unless you really crank up your noise gate pedal! You can honestly set it for whatever you like, and just play to get a sort of &#8220;glitchy&#8221; sound.</p>



<p>The <strong>Bass and Middle </strong>controls are very interesting as well, and can completely change the tone. Both knobs have a completely flat signal when positioned at noon, or the middle position. If you want to add or subtract bass of midrange, you can do so by turning either knob.</p>



<p>Both the Bass and Middle controls have a very large sweep, so you can easily blend the Spellbook with a dirty amp tone, or even an amp sim. I tried the Spellbook with amp sims, and it seems to play well with everything from Neural to STL Tones without issue.</p>



<p>You can run the Spellbook in the front of your amp, or in the FX loop as well. Both options yield a similar result, with the FX loop sounding a little bit &#8220;cleaner&#8221; to me, if that is possible with the Spellbook! If you use a noise gate, I would dial the threshold back a little bit to be able to get the &#8220;glitch&#8221; effects.</p>



<p>Multi-string tapping, and chordal tapping sounds really insane, adding overtones and ghost notes to your playing. While the Spellbook can be used for more &#8220;controlled&#8221; solos like in David&#8217;s video, the other applications are really limitless. If you tap slowly, you get the &#8220;Control note&#8221; in between your tapped notes, which sounds like a synth in a lot of ways.</p>



<p>Everything else about the Spellbook pedal design is what you expect from a &#8220;top of the line&#8221; guitar pedal. You have a standard 9V power supply that can be ran on battery or with an adapter. The volume control is no-frills, and the button is your standard stomp box.</p>



<p>The pedal enclosure is regular sized and I have to say, the artwork definitely matches the Spellbook pedal&#8217;s sound. The overall quality of the pedal is boutique, and the artwork certainly matches that high-end feel. David enjoys using unique graphics to match the vibe of his pedals, and the Spellbook is no exception.</p>



<p>But it is what is going on under the hood that makes this pedal really interesting. In fact,  <a href="http://guitarpedalbuilders.blogspot.com/p/blog-page_66.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" data-lasso-id="247807">the Howl of Flaming Death mod</a> is an integral element that ties everything together for this pedal. This mod circuit unleashes a literal flamethrower of tone. So what does it sound like? Is it as crazy as I describe?</p>

<p>As you can see in the video, all three switch modes are explored in the demo. The Spellbook can be used for guitar, as well as bass, and really any other instrument that takes a 1/4&#8221; input jack! Again, it works with audio interfaces and amps alike.</p>



<p><strong>I have been playing guitar for 30 years now, and I have never seen<em> anything</em> like the Spellbook.</strong> This is honestly something very different, and while it can sound like a simple fuzz pedal if you want, it has so much more protentional than just pedestrian use.</p>



<p>I can see the Spellbook pedal being used as a temporary effect during a live set, something to create some serious noise and chaos. It could also be used for a very interesting intro to a song, especially when mixed with delay and reverb. David Ross really got weird with this one, and it came from an interesting realization:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote has-text-align-center is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow">
<p class="has-text-align-center"><em style="font-weight: bold;"><strong>The Spellbook guitar pedal was created in part as a result of my fascination with the old <a href="https://www.boss.info/us/products/dm-2w/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" data-lasso-id="247808">Boss DM-2 Delay</a>. When you turn the Intensity and Echo controls on the <a href="https://www.guitarworld.com/features/classic-gear-boss-dm-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" data-lasso-id="247809">Boss DM-2</a> all the way up, the pedal starts to self-oscillate. You can change the pitch of the oscillation with the Repeat Rate control. I found this to be a very cool and unusual effect, but for me it had a couple of problems</strong></em></p>



<p class="has-text-align-center"><strong><em>The first was that the feedback effect would cascade and the volume would quickly get out of control. The second issue was that once the pedal would go into the feedback loop, the guitar signal would become overwhelmed and would no longer be audible. It was around August of 2020 when I said to myself “it would be cool if I could play my guitar through this…” that the idea for the Spellbook was born.&#8221;</em></strong></p>
<cite><strong>David Ross</strong></cite></blockquote>
